This section evaluates the MERIT's multi-source fusion model, directly addressing RQ2. To ensure a thorough
evaluation, we proceed from the bottom up. We first demonstrate how skill decay can affect a candidate's score, 
from there, we then test the Bayesian Fusion process, evaluating the effects of conflict resolution and 
testing to see whether MERIT does reward demonstrated evidence over claimed evidence. From there, we conduct
an algorithmic study against baseline ATS scoring engines to compare alignment to expert decisions. Finally,
we show the other half of this study, where we evaluate how MERIT handles the different personas used in that
study.
For studies involving ATS scoring engines, we specifically refer to four scoring engines. We have: a \textbf{Traditional ATS}
baseline (Keyword-Based), a \textbf{Modern AI ATS} baseline (Semantic-Based), a \textbf{MERIT (CV Only)} baseline (CV-Only), 
and a \textbf{MERIT (Full)} baseline (CV-Only, GitHub, LinkedIn). The baselines are not commercial products, they are
in-repository baselines that proxy keyword-matching and AI-assisted ATS systems. Note that we intentionally omit
rule-based engines as they are knockout-oriented, they do not rank candidates. 

Our studies are performed in isolation, meaning that we consider the successful extraction of candidate CV data.
To ensure this, we manufacture synthetic candidate JSON profiles to test various scenarios and edge cases.
This allows us to isolate the multi-source fusion and other engines from parsing noise. The pre-requisite of perfect
extraction allows us to ensure a clean mathematical baseline. This way, we can verify actual scoring logic, temporal decay
and conflict resolution are able to be demonstrated and evaluated as intended. The validity implications for this are detailed
in Section~\ref{subsec:internal-validity}.

\subsection{Skill-Decay Sensitivity Analysis (RQ2 Supplement)}
\label{subsec:study-08-skill-decay}

Prior research on skill retention shows that unused workplace skills deteriorate over time~\cite{arthur1998}.
This supporting analysis tests whether MERIT's decay parameter produces sensible rank separation 
between actively used and stale skills.
This does not calibrate $\lambda$ against Arthur et al.'s effect sizes.
\begin{equation}
    S(t) = S_0 \cdot e^{-\lambda t}
\end{equation}
Here, $S_0$ is the initial unweighted skill score and $\lambda$ is the decay constant. In MERIT, $\lambda \approx 0.25$
(half-life $t_{1/2} \approx 2.77$ years, see Section~\ref{subsec:skill-decay-modelling}). 
For two otherwise identical profiles (active vs.\ stale), a 6-year stale signal yields $S(6) = S_0 e^{-0.25 \cdot 6} \approx 0.223S_0$, 
so the stale signal retains about 22.3\% of the active value. This is sufficient for directional sensitivity, 
and we treat it as supporting evidence only.


\subsection{Study 12: Bayesian Conflict Resolution}
\label{subsec:study-12-bayesian-conflict-resolution}

The reasoning for MERIT's multi-source fusion layer is to be able to reward candidates who are able to demonstrate their skills 
with publicly available evidence. This is particularly important for extensible metrics, where said metric is tied directly
to the job description that it was derived from. We believe it to be essential to reward candidates who can prove that they are
fit for the role, rather than just claim to be fit for it. In this study, we verify the Beta-Bernoulli conjugate updating 
implementation \cite{gelman2013bayesian} with two controlled hypothetical profiles on the same extensible metric, motivated by the need to down-weight self-reported CV claims that personnel research shows are frequently embellished~\cite{henle2019_resume_fraud,dunning2004}.
The first
represents a claims-only candidate, while the second represents a demonstration-only candidate. Both candidates achieve the 
maximum score available for the signals that correspond to the category of evidence (claimed or demonstrated).

In this walkthrough, the maximum signal that signals which are claims-only can achieve is $0.8$, while the maximum signal for demonstration-only signals
is uncapped at $1.0$. We start from priors $\alpha_0 = \beta_0 = 0.1$, and the step-by-step walkthrough is given in Table~\ref{tab:bayesian-walkthrough}.

At the GitHub step (Table~\ref{tab:bayesian-walkthrough}, row~2, Figure~\ref{fig:bayesian-fusion-convergence}), the two profiles diverge sharply. The demonstration-only candidate reaches $0.624$, while the claims-only candidate falls to $0.314$, a gap of 31 percentage points. This reflects the higher source confidence assigned to demonstrated GitHub evidence ($c_{\mathrm{GH}} = 0.9$), and the fact that a zero GitHub signal actively contradicts the CV claim rather than leaving it unchallenged.

For the claims-only profile, a capped CV assertion ($s_1 = 0.8$, $c_1 = 0.5$) initially lifts the score from $0.500$ to $0.711$. This is
broadly the sort of outcome a CV-only screening pipeline might produce. After GitHub contradicts the claim, the score drops to $0.314$, and a
further LinkedIn endorsement ($s_3 = 0.8$, $c_3 = 0.2$) only recovers it to $0.369$. Additional self-report cannot substitute for missing
public work samples.

The demonstration-only profile follows the reverse pattern. An empty CV ($s_{\mathrm{CV}} = 0$) briefly pulls the score down to $0.148$
before GitHub ingestion, after which strong demonstrated evidence lifts it to $0.624$ and the final fused score settles at $0.555$. When we
enable multi-source fusion in this way, MERIT rewards verifiable work and does not treat self-report as sufficient when repositories
contradict it. This bias towards demonstrated evidence is intentional. Self-reported CVs are embellished frequently, and a system that
treated claimed and demonstrated signals equally would be straightforward to game. MERIT is therefore designed to favour candidates who
can back up their skills with public work, while still recording uncertainty (rather than a hard rejection) when a candidate has no
demonstrated evidence to show.

\subsection{Study 01: Comparative Engine and Source Ablation}
\label{subsec:study-01-comparative-ablation}

To evaluate the benefits and drawbacks of MERIT's multi-source fusion model, we compare it against three other baseline screening engines on
the same ten synthetic personas (\texttt{evaluation/01-comparative\_study/}).
The first is \textbf{Traditional ATS}, an in-repository keyword-matching baseline representing the keyword-matching ATS category in
Section~\ref{sec:automated-screening}.
It scores candidates solely on the count of matching keywords in their CV.
The second is \textbf{Modern AI ATS}, an in-repository semantic baseline representing the AI-assisted ATS category in Section~\ref{sec:automated-screening}.
It computes cosine similarities between CV embeddings and Job Description (JD) embeddings using Sentence-BERT (SBERT)~\cite{reimers2019sentence}.
Neither baseline is a commercial ATS platform.
We also run \textbf{MERIT (CV Only)} on CV data alone, and \textbf{MERIT (Full)}, which considers CV, GitHub, and LinkedIn evidence.

\subsubsection{Jordan Smith: hidden gem}

Jordan Smith is a junior developer with only one year of formal experience on his CV.
Traditional ATS and Modern AI ATS rank him \#10 and \#9 respectively because they consider CV text alone.
What is not mentioned on his CV, however, is extensive public work on GitHub in Java and TypeScript, two core technologies in the study JD.

Under MERIT (Full), Jordan rises from rank \#10 to rank \#2.
He has the strongest demonstrated evidence in the batch, and MERIT (Full) is the only engine that rewards it.
By shifting from unverified claims towards verifiable work samples, MERIT can surface candidates who would otherwise be lost in the
traditional ``Resume Black Hole''~\cite{fuller2021hiddenworkers}.

\subsubsection{Carl Corp: proprietary data gap}

Carl Corp illustrates the cost of depending on public evidence.
Most of his commits are in private repositories, so his GitHub profile is essentially empty.
He receives weak GitHub signals and cannot demonstrate the programming languages required by the JD.
Under MERIT (Full) he falls from rank \#2 (Modern AI ATS) to rank \#10.

This ``Proprietary Data Gap'' can unfairly hinder candidates who cannot share code publicly, while favouring juniors with active open-source
histories.
MERIT therefore introduces a trade-off between verifiable screening and accessibility of evidence.

\subsubsection{Vince Vault: identity squatting}
\label{subsubsec:identity-mismatch-resolution}

When MERIT ingests multiple sources, candidates may try to game the system with false evidence.
We refer to this as \textbf{identity squatting}.

Vince Vault is a fraudulent persona used to test this case.
On his CV he claims ten years of experience as a Lead Software Architect and presents himself as a Java expert.
He does not have a genuine Java portfolio on his own GitHub.
Instead, he submits a link to another candidate's GitHub account so that their demonstrated work appears to support his application.
Traditional ATS ranks him \#1 and Modern AI ATS ranks him \#4 because neither engine verifies ownership of the linked repository.

MERIT (CV Only) still ranks Vince at \#4 on CV text alone.
Under MERIT (Full), the Integrity Layer compares the name on the CV with the GitHub account metadata, detects the mismatch, and applies a flat veto penalty.
His rank falls to \#9.
Buzz Ward shows a smaller but related pattern: he drops from rank \#3 to rank \#6 when GitHub does not corroborate his stuffed CV claims.

Identity squatting is rare in practice, but it is an important edge case for any multi-source screening design.
